MODULE 1 · THE RESTORING RULE

Why a system oscillates instead of simply returning and stopping

Start with a picture in your head

Imagine a minecart sitting at the bottom of a shallow valley. Nudge it to the right. Gravity now has a component pulling it back toward the lowest point. The farther up the slope you push it, the stronger the tendency to return. If the valley is locally shaped like a parabola, that restoring tendency is proportional to displacement.

A spring does the same thing in one dimension. Stretch it by x and the spring pulls with F = −kx . The minus sign is not decoration. It is a directional statement. Rightward displacement produces leftward force. Leftward displacement produces rightward force.

Step 1 · Hooke's law F = −kx
combine with Newton's second law ↓
Step 2 · Dynamics m x¨ = −kx
divide by m ↓
Step 3 · SHM equation x¨ + (k/m)x = 0
identify ω² = k/m ↓
Step 4 · Standard form x¨ + ω²x = 0

That last line is the mathematical fingerprint of ideal SHM. If acceleration can be written as a = −ω²x , the motion is simple harmonic.

Why it overshoots equilibrium

At maximum displacement the mass is instantaneously at rest, but the restoring force is largest. It accelerates inward. By the time it reaches equilibrium, the restoring force has dropped to zero—but the mass now has maximum speed.

With nothing instantaneously stopping that momentum, it flies straight through equilibrium. The force then reverses and begins braking it.

Analogy: running down a hill

If you run downhill toward the lowest point, you do not stop merely because the slope becomes flat. You have acquired speed. You continue up the other side until the slope removes that kinetic energy. SHM is the same perpetual exchange between “being displaced” and “being in motion”.

What counts as SHM?

a = −9x SHM linear and inward
a = +9x not SHM pushes away from equilibrium
F = −12x² not exact SHM nonlinear in displacement

DERIVATION ROOM

Where does ω = √(k/m) actually come from?

Start from m x¨ = −kx . Dividing by m gives x¨ = −(k/m)x . Compare it with the generic SHM relation x¨ = −ω²x . The coefficients of x must therefore match:

ω² = k/m
ω = √(k/m)

The scaling now makes physical sense. A larger spring constant k means a stronger restoring force for the same displacement, so the system turns around more aggressively and oscillates faster.

A larger mass m means greater inertia. The same spring force changes its velocity more slowly, so the oscillation takes longer.

MINDMAP · MODULE 1

ACTIVE RECALL

Why must the minus sign appear in F = −kx ?

Because the force must oppose the displacement. Without the minus sign, the force would push the object farther away from equilibrium rather than restore it.

ACTIVE RECALL

If mass quadruples while k stays fixed, what happens to the period?

Since T = 2π√(m/k) , multiplying m by 4 multiplies T by √4 = 2 . The period doubles.
Worked example · build the motion from the force law

A 0.40 kg mass is attached to a 90 N/m spring. Find its angular frequency and period.

  1. Write the dynamical relation: m x¨ = −kx .
  2. Recognise ω² = k/m = 90/0.40 = 225 s⁻² .
  3. Therefore ω = 15 rad s⁻¹ .
  4. Then T = 2π/ω = 0.419 s .

Reasonableness check: a fairly stiff spring and modest mass should oscillate quickly, so a sub-second period is plausible.

MODULE 2 · THE SINUSOIDAL SOLUTION

Why cosine is not an arbitrary choice

The differential equation is asking for a self-reversing function

The equation x¨ + ω²x = 0 says something remarkably specific: differentiate displacement twice and you should recover the original function multiplied by a negative constant.

Sine and cosine have exactly that property.

Assume x = A cos(ωt + φ)
differentiate once ↓
Velocity v = x˙ = −Aω sin(ωt + φ)
differentiate again ↓
Acceleration a = x¨ = −Aω² cos(ωt + φ)
recognise x again ↓
Therefore a = −ω²x

That is why cosine appears naturally. Its second derivative reproduces the same waveform with the opposite sign.

Amplitude, frequency and phase play different roles

In x(t)=A cos(ωt+φ) , the amplitude A tells you how far from equilibrium the oscillator can travel.

The angular frequency ω tells you how quickly phase advances through a cycle. The phase constant φ tells you where in that cycle the clock starts.

Analogy: a circular race track

Imagine phase as your angular location on a circular track. ω tells you how quickly you run around it. φ tells you where you were standing when the stopwatch began. The observed SHM displacement is simply the shadow of your circular position projected onto one axis.

UNDERGRADUATE DERIVATION

Solving the differential equation with exponentials

Suppose x = e^{rt} . Then x¨ = r²e^{rt} . Substitution into x¨ + ω²x = 0 gives

r²e^{rt} + ω²e^{rt} = 0
e^{rt} ≠ 0 ↓
r² + ω² = 0
solve the characteristic equation ↓
r = ±iω
superpose both solutions ↓
x = C₁e^{iωt} + C₂e^{-iωt}

Using Euler's identity, e^{iθ}=cosθ+i sinθ , the same solution can be rewritten entirely in real form:

x = C cos(ωt) + D sin(ωt)

The constants C and D can then be repackaged as an amplitude and phase:

x = A cos(ωt + φ)

So the trigonometric and complex-exponential representations are not different physics. They are different coordinate systems for the same two-dimensional solution space.

MINDMAP · MODULE 2

ACTIVE RECALL

Why can sine and cosine both describe SHM?

They differ only by a phase shift. For example, sin θ = cos(θ − π/2) . Changing from sine to cosine does not change the physical motion if the phase constant is adjusted consistently.

ACTIVE RECALL

What does φ physically control?

φ selects where in the oscillation cycle the system is at t=0. It therefore determines the initial displacement and, together with the sign of the sine term, the initial direction of motion.

MODULE 3 · POSITION, VELOCITY & ACCELERATION

Three views of the same oscillator

Imagine three observers watching the same mass

The first observer records where the mass is. The second asks how quickly that position is changing. The third asks how quickly the velocity itself is changing. Those observers measure x, v and a.

position x = A cos(ωt + φ)
differentiate ↓
velocity v = −ωA sin(ωt + φ)
differentiate ↓
acceleration a = −ω²A cos(ωt + φ)
substitute x ↓
a = −ω²x

Notice what differentiation does. Each differentiation introduces a factor of ω, so more rapid oscillations produce disproportionately larger acceleration.

The amplitude of position is A . The amplitude of velocity is ωA . The amplitude of acceleration is ω²A .

CIRCULAR-PROJECTION MODEL

Eliminate time: deriving speed directly from position

Sometimes a problem gives position but does not give time. Instead of solving for phase first, eliminate the phase angle.

x/A = cos θ
v/(ωA) = −sin θ
square both equations ↓
x²/A² = cos²θ
v²/(ω²A²) = sin²θ
add and use sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 ↓
v² = ω²(A² − x²)

This equation encodes the geometry of the circular model and the energy structure of SHM at the same time.

MODULE 4 · ENERGY

SHM as an endless exchange between two stores

Imagine energy being poured between two buckets

One bucket is spring potential energy. The other is kinetic energy. In an ideal frictionless oscillator the total amount of water never changes. It simply moves between the two buckets.

spring potential U = ½kx²
kinetic K = ½mv²
total E = K + U = ½kA²

At x = ±A , the mass is momentarily stationary. Therefore K = 0 and all energy is potential.

At x = 0 , the spring is instantaneously unstretched relative to equilibrium. Therefore U = 0 and all the mechanical energy is kinetic.

Why energy provides another route to v(x)

Set total energy equal to kinetic plus potential:

½kA² = ½mv² + ½kx²

Multiply by two:

kA² = mv² + kx²

Rearrange:

mv² = k(A² − x²)

Since k/m = ω² ,

v² = ω²(A² − x²)

This is the exact same relation obtained earlier from trigonometry. That agreement is important: the calculus, geometry and energy descriptions are all different views of the same motion.

A useful undergraduate check: prove that total energy is constant

Begin with E = ½mv² + ½kx² and differentiate with respect to time:

dE/dt = mv(dv/dt) + kx(dx/dt)

Since dx/dt = v and dv/dt = a ,

dE/dt = v(ma + kx)

But SHM obeys ma = −kx . Therefore

dE/dt = 0

Energy conservation is therefore not merely an assumption pasted onto the motion. It follows directly from the conservative restoring force.

MINDMAP · MODULE 4

ACTIVE RECALL

Why is velocity zero at x = ±A?

At a turning point, U = ½kA² already equals the total energy. No energy remains for kinetic energy, so K = 0 and v = 0.

ACTIVE RECALL

Why do K and U vary twice as rapidly as x?

Energy contains squared sinusoidal quantities. For example, U ∝ cos²(ωt+φ) , and cos²θ = [1 + cos(2θ)]/2 . The oscillating part therefore has angular frequency 2ω.

MODULE 5 · ANGULAR SHM

The same mathematical machine in rotational form

Torsion oscillators

A twisted wire or torsion fibre provides a restoring torque proportional to angular displacement:

τ = −κθ

Rotational Newton's second law is

Iθ¨ = τ

Combining them:

Iθ¨ = −κθ
divide by I ↓
θ¨ + (κ/I)θ = 0
compare with θ¨ + ω²θ = 0 ↓
ω = √(κ/I)

Structurally this is identical to the spring result ω = √(k/m) . Translational mass m has simply been replaced by rotational inertia I, while k has been replaced by the torsional constant κ.

Why a pendulum is only approximately SHM

The tangential restoring force on a simple pendulum is

Fₜ = −mg sinθ

The obstacle is the sine. Exact SHM requires the restoring term to be directly proportional to displacement.

For small angles measured in radians,

sinθ ≈ θ

The nonlinear equation then becomes approximately linear:

θ¨ + (g/L)θ ≈ 0

Hence

ω = √(g/L)
T = 2π√(L/g)

Physical pendulum: why rotational inertia matters

A real rigid body swinging about a pivot cannot generally be treated as a point mass at one radius. Its mass is distributed through space. That distribution enters through the moment of inertia I.

For a centre of mass located distance h from the pivot, the gravitational torque is approximately

τ ≈ −mghθ

Hence

Iθ¨ = −mghθ
rearrange ↓
θ¨ + (mgh/I)θ = 0
identify ω² ↓
ω² = mgh/I
convert to period ↓
T = 2π√(I/mgh)

ACTIVE RECALL

Why must θ be measured in radians in sinθ ≈ θ?

The Taylor expansion is sinθ = θ − θ³/6 + ... only when θ is expressed in radians. Degrees introduce an additional conversion factor.

ACTIVE RECALL

Why is the ideal pendulum period independent of bob mass?

Increasing the mass increases the gravitational restoring force and the inertia by the same factor. The mass cancels from the equation of motion.

MODULE 6 · DAMPING

How real oscillators gradually forget their motion

Drag attacks velocity, not position

A common damping model is

Fd = −bv

The minus sign means the drag force always opposes motion. Combine spring force and damping:

m x¨ = −kx − bx˙

Rearranging gives

m x¨ + b x˙ + kx = 0

The new x˙ term changes the roots of the differential equation. The result depends on how large b is compared with 2√(mk) .

Three damping regimes

b < 2√mk underdamped oscillates while decaying
b = 2√mk critical fastest return without oscillation
b > 2√mk overdamped slow non-oscillatory return
Visualise three doors

An underdamped door swings past the closed position several times. A critically damped door reaches closed position quickly without overshooting. An overdamped door crawls slowly toward closed because its damper resists motion too strongly.

Why amplitude and energy decay at different exponential rates

In the underdamped case the displacement envelope behaves as

A(t) = A₀e^{-bt/2m}

But oscillator energy scales as amplitude squared:

E ∝ A²

Therefore

E(t) = E₀e^{-bt/m}

The exponent has doubled. Energy therefore falls twice as rapidly in the exponent as amplitude.

Consequently,

t½,amplitude = 2m ln2 / b
t½,energy = m ln2 / b

MODULE 7 · FORCED OSCILLATIONS & RESONANCE

When an external rhythm repeatedly feeds the oscillator

Imagine pushing a swing

One push does not define resonance. Resonance is about repeated timing. Push at random phases and some pushes add energy while others remove it. Push in step with the motion and each push can reinforce the previous one.

A driven oscillator obeys

m x¨ + b x˙ + kx = F₀ cos(ωt)

The left side describes the oscillator. The right side describes an external periodic driver.

After transient motion dies away, the steady-state oscillator follows the driving frequency ω. Its natural frequency still matters because it determines how strongly the system responds.

Resonance is an energy-transfer phenomenon

For weak damping, the response becomes particularly large when the driving frequency lies near the natural frequency.

ωdrive ≈ ω₀

This does not mean the oscillator somehow "prefers" a frequency psychologically. It means the forcing remains favourably phased so that positive work is repeatedly done on the oscillator.

Analogy: adding coins to a moving minecart

Add momentum at precisely the right points in each trip and successive contributions accumulate. Add them at the wrong time and one contribution can undo part of another.

RESONANCE LAB

The steady-state response formula

For m x¨ + b x˙ + kx = F₀ cosωt , the steady-state displacement amplitude can be written

X(ω) = F₀ / √[(k − mω²)² + (bω)²]

The denominator contains two competing effects. The term k − mω² represents the mismatch between restoring stiffness and inertial response.

Near k ≈ mω² , that part becomes small. If damping is also weak, the denominator becomes small and therefore the amplitude becomes large.

Damping prevents the amplitude from diverging. Increasing damping lowers the peak and broadens it, making the oscillator less frequency-selective.

MINDMAP · MODULE 7

ACTIVE RECALL

At what frequency does the long-term response oscillate?

At the driving frequency. The system's natural frequency controls the magnitude and phase of the response, but the steady-state particular solution follows the external forcing frequency.

ACTIVE RECALL

What does increasing damping do to resonance?

The resonance peak becomes lower and broader because more energy is dissipated per cycle.
