MODULE 1 · THE RESTORING RULE

Why a system oscillates instead of simply returning and stopping

Start with a picture in your head

Imagine a minecart sitting at the bottom of a shallow valley. Nudge it to the right. Gravity now has a component pulling it back toward the lowest point. The farther up the slope you push it, the stronger the tendency to return. If the valley is locally shaped like a parabola, that restoring tendency is proportional to displacement.

A spring does the same thing in one dimension. Stretch it by x and the spring pulls with F = −kx . The minus sign is not decoration: it is the directional statement that the force points opposite the displacement. Rightward displacement produces leftward force; leftward displacement produces rightward force.

Step 1 · Hooke's law F = −kx
combine with Newton's second law ↓
Step 2 · Dynamics m x¨ = −kx
divide by m ↓
Step 3 · SHM equation x¨ + (k/m)x = 0
identify ω² = k/m ↓
Step 4 · Standard form x¨ + ω²x = 0

That last line is the mathematical fingerprint of ideal SHM. If the acceleration can be written as a = −ω²x , the motion is simple harmonic.

Why it overshoots equilibrium

At maximum displacement the mass is instantaneously at rest, but the restoring force is largest. It accelerates inward. By the time it reaches equilibrium, the restoring force has dropped to zero — but the mass now has maximum speed. With nothing instantaneously stopping that momentum, it flies straight through equilibrium. The force then reverses and begins braking it.

Analogy: running down a hill

If you run downhill toward the lowest point, you do not stop merely because the slope becomes flat. You have acquired speed. You continue up the other side until the slope removes that kinetic energy. SHM is the same perpetual exchange between "being displaced" and "being in motion".

What counts as SHM?

a = −9x SHM linear and inward
a = +9x not SHM pushes away from equilibrium
F = −12x² not exact SHM nonlinear in displacement

DERIVATION ROOM

Where does ω = √(k/m) actually come from?

Start from m x¨ = −kx . Dividing by m gives x¨ = −(k/m)x . Compare it with the generic SHM relation x¨ = −ω²x . The coefficients of x must match, so ω² = k/m and therefore ω = √(k/m) .

The scaling now makes physical sense. A larger k means a stronger restoring force for the same displacement, so the system turns around more aggressively and oscillates faster. A larger m means more inertia, so the same spring force changes the velocity more slowly and the oscillation is slower.

MINDMAP · MODULE 1

ACTIVE RECALL

Why must the minus sign appear in F = −kx ?

Because the force must oppose the displacement. Without the minus sign, the force would push the object farther away from equilibrium rather than restore it.

ACTIVE RECALL

If mass quadruples while k stays fixed, what happens to the period?

Since T = 2π√(m/k) , multiplying m by 4 multiplies T by √4 = 2 . The period doubles.
Worked example · build the motion from the force law

A 0.40 kg mass is attached to a 90 N/m spring. Find its angular frequency and period.

  1. Write the dynamical relation: m x¨ = −kx .
  2. Recognise ω² = k/m = 90/0.40 = 225 s⁻² .
  3. Therefore ω = 15 rad s⁻¹ .
  4. Then T = 2π/ω = 0.419 s .

Reasonableness check: a fairly stiff spring and modest mass should oscillate quickly, so a sub-second period is plausible.

MODULE 2 · THE SINUSOIDAL SOLUTION

Why cosine is not an arbitrary choice

The differential equation is asking for a self-reversing function

The equation x¨ + ω²x = 0 says: "differentiate the displacement twice and you should get the original function back with a minus sign and a factor of ω² ." Sines and cosines have exactly that property.

Assume x = A cos(ωt + φ)
differentiate once ↓
Velocity v = x˙ = −Aω sin(ωt + φ)
differentiate again ↓
Acceleration a = x¨ = −Aω² cos(ωt + φ)
recognise x again ↓
Therefore a = −ω²x

That is why a cosine is so natural here: the second derivative reproduces the same shape with opposite sign.

Amplitude, frequency and phase play different roles

In x(t)=A cos(ωt+φ) , the amplitude A tells you how far from equilibrium the oscillator can travel. The angular frequency ω tells you how fast the phase advances through the cycle. The phase constant φ tells you where in the cycle the clock starts.

Analogy: a circular race track

Imagine phase as your angular location on a circular track. ω is how quickly you run around it; φ is where you were standing when the stopwatch started. The observed SHM displacement is the shadow of your circular position projected onto one axis.

Frequency dictionary

f = 1/T ω = 2πf T = 2π/ω

UNDERGRADUATE MATHS VIEW

The characteristic-equation route

For x¨ + ω²x = 0 , try an exponential trial solution x=e^{rt} . Then x¨=r²e^{rt} , so the ODE becomes (r²+ω²)e^{rt}=0 . Since e^{rt} is not zero, r²+ω²=0 , giving r=±iω . Euler's formula then converts the complex exponentials into sine and cosine.

x¨+ω²x=0
→
r²+ω²=0
→
r=±iω
→
x=C cosωt + D sinωt

The form A cos(ωt+φ) and the form C cosωt + D sinωt are mathematically equivalent. The first is intuitive for amplitude and phase; the second is often convenient when applying initial conditions.

Why can sine and cosine both describe the same oscillator?

They differ only by a phase shift: sin θ = cos(θ−π/2) . Changing the phase constant lets either function represent the same physical motion.

ACTIVE RECALL

What does increasing φ do physically?

It changes the starting point within the oscillation cycle without changing the amplitude or natural frequency. It shifts the motion horizontally in time.

MODULE 3 · POSITION, VELOCITY & ACCELERATION

Three waveforms, one moving object

Visualise the cycle as a conversation between position and momentum

At a turning point, the mass has travelled as far as it can: |x|=A . Its speed is zero, but the restoring acceleration is maximal. At equilibrium, the opposite is true: x=0 , so the restoring acceleration vanishes, yet the mass is moving fastest. Position and velocity therefore peak a quarter-cycle apart. Acceleration is exactly opposite displacement because a=−ω²x .

MODULE 4 · ENERGY

Oscillation as energy sloshing between two stores

Think of two chests sharing one fixed treasure

In an ideal frictionless oscillator, the total mechanical energy is constant. The "treasure" moves between elastic potential energy and kinetic energy. Near the turning points almost all of it is stored in the spring. Near equilibrium almost all of it is carried as motion.

spring potential U = ½kx²
kinetic K = ½mv²
total E = K + U = ½kA²

The constant ½kA² is the total energy because at a turning point x=±A and v=0 .

Derive the speed-position relation from energy

½mv² + ½kx² = ½kA²
multiply by 2 ↓
mv² = k(A²−x²)
use k/m = ω² ↓
v² = ω²(A²−x²)

Notice the same formula appeared in Module 3 from trigonometry. That is a valuable consistency check: kinematics and energy are telling the same story from different viewpoints.

MINDMAP · MODULE 4

ACTIVE RECALL

At x = A/2, what fraction of the total energy is potential?

U/E = x²/A² = (1/2)² = 1/4 . So 25% is potential and 75% is kinetic.

ACTIVE RECALL

Why does energy oscillate at twice the displacement frequency?

Because U∝x²∝cos²(ωt+φ) and K∝sin²(ωt+φ) . Squaring removes the sign and produces terms involving cos 2ωt .
Worked example · speed from energy

A 0.50 kg mass on an 80 N/m spring oscillates with amplitude 0.20 m. Find its speed at x = 0.12 m.

  1. ω=√(k/m)=√(80/0.50)=12.65 rad s⁻¹ .
  2. v=ω√(A²−x²) .
  3. v=12.65√(0.20²−0.12²)=2.02 m s⁻¹ .

The equation gives speed. The sign of velocity requires information about the direction of motion.

MODULE 5 · ANGULAR SHM & PENDULUMS

The same architecture, rotated

Torsion oscillator

A torsion wire behaves like a rotational spring. Twist it through angle θ and it provides restoring torque τ=−κθ .

τ = Iθ¨
Iθ¨ = −κθ
θ¨ + (κ/I)θ = 0

Therefore ω=√(κ/I) and T=2π√(I/κ) .

Simple pendulum

The exact tangential restoring force is −mg sinθ . That is nonlinear. The pendulum only becomes approximately SHM for sufficiently small angular displacement.

mLθ¨ = −mg sinθ
small angle: sinθ ≈ θ ↓
Lθ¨ ≈ −gθ
θ¨ + (g/L)θ = 0

So ω=√(g/L) and T=2π√(L/g) .

Physical pendulum

For an extended rigid body pivoted a distance h from its centre of mass, the restoring torque is approximately −mghθ .

Iθ¨ = −mghθ
T = 2π√(I/mgh)

The simple pendulum is recovered if I=mL² and h=L .

WHY THE SMALL-ANGLE APPROXIMATION WORKS

Linearisation turns a nonlinear pendulum into SHM

The Taylor expansion is sinθ = θ − θ³/3! + θ⁵/5! − … . For small |θ| in radians, higher powers become rapidly smaller, so sinθ≈θ . The moment we make that approximation, the equation becomes linear in θ and matches the SHM template.

MINDMAP · MODULE 5

ACTIVE RECALL

Why does the simple-pendulum period not contain mass?

The gravitational restoring force scales with m, but the inertia also scales with m. The factors cancel in the equation of motion.

ACTIVE RECALL

Why must θ be measured in radians for sinθ ≈ θ?

The Taylor series of sine has first term θ only when θ is dimensionless in radians. Degree measure introduces an extra conversion factor.

MODULE 6 · DAMPING

Real oscillators lose memory of their amplitude

Add a force that resists motion

A common model for viscous damping is F_d=−bv . It opposes velocity rather than displacement.

spring + damping m x¨ = −kx − bx˙
collect terms ↓
m x¨ + b x˙ + kx = 0

For the underdamped case, the oscillator still crosses equilibrium repeatedly, but the amplitude envelope shrinks exponentially: x=A₀e^{-bt/2m}cos(ω_d t+φ) .

ω_d = √(k/m − b²/4m²) .

Three damping regimes

Underdamped

Oscillates while amplitude decays.

Critical damping

Returns to equilibrium as fast as possible without oscillating.

Overdamped

Returns without oscillation, but more slowly than critical damping.

Analogy: closing a door

Too little damping and the door swings back and forth. Too much damping and it crawls shut. Critical damping is the fastest clean return.

ENERGY DECAY

Why energy falls twice as fast in the exponent

The amplitude envelope scales as A(t)∝e^{-bt/2m} . Mechanical energy scales as amplitude squared, E∝A² , so E(t)∝e^{-bt/m} . The exponent doubles because the amplitude itself is squared.

MINDMAP · MODULE 6

ACTIVE RECALL

Which variable does viscous damping oppose?

Velocity: F_d=−bv . The sign ensures the damping force points opposite the direction of motion.

ACTIVE RECALL

If amplitude falls as e^{-γt}, how does energy scale?

Since E∝A² , energy falls as e^{-2γt} .

MODULE 7 · FORCED OSCILLATIONS & RESONANCE

When the outside world keeps pushing

Add a periodic driver

Suppose the oscillator is driven by F(t)=F₀cos(ωt) . The equation becomes

m x¨ + b x˙ + kx = F₀ cos(ωt)

After transient motion dies away, the steady-state response oscillates at the driving frequency. The amplitude depends strongly on how close that drive is to the system's natural frequency.

Undergraduate extension

steady-state amplitude X(ω)=F₀ / √[(k−mω²)²+(bω)²]
phase lag tanδ = bω/(k−mω²)

Imagine pushing a swing

A swing has a preferred rhythm. Push at random times and much of your effort cancels previous motion. Push once every natural cycle and each push arrives with the correct phase to add energy. The amplitude grows until damping balances the average power supplied by the driver.

Resonance is selective energy transfer

It is not merely "large vibration". It is the condition under which a periodic force couples efficiently to a natural mode.

Real examples include vibration isolation, building sway, musical instruments, atomic-force microscopes, MEMS resonators and driven RLC circuits.

RESONANCE LAB

PHASE MATTERS

The driver does work only when force and velocity cooperate

Instantaneous power is P=Fv . If force and velocity tend to have the same sign, the driver adds energy; if they oppose, the driver removes energy. Near resonance the phase relation allows positive energy input over a cycle to be especially effective.

MINDMAP · MODULE 7

ACTIVE RECALL

After transients die away, what frequency does the driven oscillator use?

The driving frequency. The natural frequency still shapes the response amplitude and phase, but the steady-state motion follows the driver.

ACTIVE RECALL

What does stronger damping do to the resonance peak?

It lowers and broadens the peak, reducing the maximum steady-state amplitude and making the response less sharply frequency-selective.
